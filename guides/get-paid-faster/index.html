How to Get Paid Faster: Deposits, Invoices and Late Payments for Contractors

By Jeff, Jobsite Forms Company · Updated 2026-10-27 · 5 min read

Doing the work is the part most contractors are good at. Getting paid for it on time is where a lot of them struggle. The job is done, the customer is happy, and somehow the check is three weeks late. Meanwhile you've already paid for material, fuel and your crew.

Most slow payments aren't caused by bad customers. They come from a fuzzy agreement, a vague invoice, or nobody following up. All three are fixable. This guide covers what to do before, during and after the job so money comes in faster.

Before the job: set the terms in writing

The single biggest thing you can do is agree on how and when you'll be paid before you start. Put it in a simple written contract or a signed estimate. It should spell out:

  • The price and exactly what's included and excluded.
  • The deposit: how much and when it's due.
  • The payment schedule: what gets paid at which stage.
  • How changes are handled: extra work is priced and approved in writing first. Our change order guide covers this.
  • Payment methods you accept.
  • What happens if a payment is late: a late fee if allowed in your state, and that work may pause.

When the terms are written and signed, a late payment stops being an awkward personal conversation. You're just pointing at what you both agreed to.

Take a deposit

A deposit does three things. It covers your up-front material costs, it confirms the customer is serious, and it puts you ahead instead of behind on cash.

  • Size it to cover at least the material and start-up costs for the job.
  • Ask for it at signing, and don't order special material until it clears.
  • Check your state's rules. Some states limit how big a deposit can be on home improvement work, or require specific wording in the contract. A quick look at your state contractor board's website will tell you.

If a customer refuses any deposit on a job with real material costs, that tells you something about how the rest of the job may go.

Bill in milestones, not all at the end

On anything longer than a few days, don't wait until the end to get paid. Break the price into stages tied to visible progress, for example:

StageExample share
Deposit at signingA set amount or percent
Rough-in or framing completeNext payment
Drywall, cabinets or main install completeNext payment
Final walkthrough and punch list doneBalance

Tie each payment to something the customer can see. "When the cabinets are installed" is clear. "Halfway through" invites an argument. Keep the final payment small enough that a punch-list dispute doesn't hold up most of your money.

Make your invoices easy to pay

An invoice that confuses people sits on the kitchen counter. A good one gets paid. Include:

  • Your business name, phone and email, and the customer's name and job address.
  • An invoice number and the date.
  • A short, clear description of the work or the milestone.
  • Approved change orders listed separately, each with its own number.
  • The total, payments already received, and the balance due.
  • A specific due date, like "Due by March 14," instead of "Net 30."
  • How to pay, with every method spelled out.

Then send it right away. The best time to invoice is the day the milestone is done, while the customer is looking at good work. Every day you wait, the job feels less urgent to them.

Make paying easy

The more ways people can pay, the fewer excuses there are. Many customers would rather pay by card or a payment app than write a check. Card processing has fees, so build that cost into your prices instead of eating it. Some contractors add a card surcharge, but the rules for that vary by state and by card network, so check before you do it.

Even better: collect the final payment in person at the walkthrough. Walk the job together, sign off on the punch list, and take payment on the spot.

Keep records that back you up

When a payment turns into a dispute, records win. Keep:

  • The signed contract or estimate.
  • Signed change orders.
  • A short daily log of who was on site and what got done.
  • Before, during and after photos with dates.
  • Texts and emails about approvals and schedule changes.

You'll rarely need all of it. When you do need it, nothing else matters as much.

When a payment is late: a calm ladder

Have a plan before it happens, so you don't react out of frustration. Each step is a little firmer than the last:

  1. Day 1 past due: friendly reminder. A short text or email. "Hi, just a reminder that invoice 1042 for the kitchen was due yesterday. You can pay by card at this link or by check. Thanks!" Most late payments end here. People forget.
  2. About a week late: a phone call. Ask if there's a problem with the work or the invoice. Sometimes there is, and fixing it gets you paid. If it's a cash problem, agree on a specific date or a short payment plan, and confirm it in writing.
  3. Two to three weeks late: a firm written notice. Restate the amount, the original due date, any late fee in your contract, and a new deadline. Stay professional. No threats you won't follow through on.
  4. Still unpaid: a final demand letter. Say what you'll do next if it isn't paid by a date: file a lien, go to small claims, or send it to collections.
  5. Use your legal options. Mechanic's lien rights can be powerful, but they come with strict deadlines and notice rules that vary a lot by state. Some states require a preliminary notice early in the job just to keep lien rights. Learn your state's rules before you need them, and talk to a local attorney for anything big.

While you're working down the ladder, it's generally reasonable to pause further work on that job if your contract allows it. Don't keep pouring labor into a job that isn't paying.

Small habits that speed up payment

  • Invoice the same day a milestone is done.
  • Confirm the customer received the invoice.
  • Thank people who pay on time. It sounds minor, but it builds the habit.
  • Track every open invoice in one place, with how many days past due each one is.
  • Look at that list every week, at the same time.

Quick recap

  • Agree on payment terms in writing before you start.
  • Take a deposit and bill in milestones tied to visible progress.
  • Send clear invoices the same day, with a specific due date.
  • Follow up on a set schedule, getting a little firmer each time.
  • Know your state's lien deadlines before you need them.

Frequently asked questions

How fast should I send an invoice?

The same day the work or milestone is done. Customers pay fastest while the finished job is fresh in their mind.

Can I charge a late fee?

Often, if it's in your signed contract and allowed in your state. Some states cap late fees or interest, so check before you add one.

General information, not legal advice. Deposit limits, late fees, surcharges and lien rules vary by state. Check your state's rules or talk to a local attorney.
